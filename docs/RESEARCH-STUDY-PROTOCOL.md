# Read this first: timing and what "required" means

**The conference is on Saturday 21 November 2026.** The design depends on a baseline questionnaire completed *before* the conference starts (09:00 on the day). No data can be collected until a university research ethics committee has approved the study, so:

- **If ethics approval is already in place**, record it on the Research study page of the committee dashboard, switch the study on, and email the invitation the same day. Expect a lower baseline response with under 48 hours to go (see the pessimistic scenario in section 9).
- **If approval is not yet in place**, do not run the study at INSPIRE 2026. Collecting the baseline afterwards ("retrospective pre-test") would remove the study's main strength and would not survive peer review as the primary design. The build is tied to the study settings, not to this year, so it can run unchanged at INSPIRE 2027 and at partner student conferences, and running it at two or more conferences would also fix the study's largest weakness (one site).

**"Required" pre-conference survey.** A research study cannot be compulsory: a requirement to take part would make consent invalid, and ethics committees will reject it. The build therefore makes *answering the invitation* required, not *taking part*:

- every invited delegate sees the invitation in My Hub on each visit, and a "to do" item in their checklist, until they choose **Take part** or **No thanks**;
- "No thanks" is one tap, as prominent as "Take part", and is never asked again;
- "Ask me later" hides it until their next visit;
- nothing (registration, the ticket, presentations, judging, prizes, workshop places, certificates) checks the answer, and the committee cannot see who said yes or no.

This gives every delegate a prompt they have to act on, which raises response rates, while consent stays voluntary.

# 2. Background and rationale

Medical students' engagement with research is linked to later academic careers, and widening access to research opportunities is a stated priority for academic medicine. Student-led research conferences are now common in the UK: they give students a place to present, hear researchers and meet potential supervisors. They are also cheap to run and reach students who have no research on their course or no mentor. Whether they do more than leave students feeling enthused is not known.

**Theoretical framework.** The study uses Social Cognitive Career Theory (SCCT) [1], which has been applied to clinical research careers and to medical students' research self-efficacy [2, 3]. In SCCT, self-efficacy (belief in one's ability to do research tasks) and contextual supports and barriers (here, perceived access to opportunities and mentors) shape goals (intentions), which shape actions. A conference could act on each link: talks and workshops may build self-efficacy, conversations with academics may make opportunities feel accessible, and both may turn intentions into action. The study measures self-efficacy, perceived access, career interest (as an indicator of goals) and actions in the same person over time.

# 3. What is already known, and the gaps this study addresses

A targeted search (PubMed/PMC, publisher sites and Google Scholar, November 2026) looked for evaluations of student-led and undergraduate medical conferences, interventions to increase research engagement, and measures of research self-efficacy in medical students. It was not a systematic review. Before the manuscript is written, a formal scoping search should be run (suggested strategy in Appendix A), because a study published in the last few months could change the novelty claims below.

| Ali et al. 2026 [7], student sport and exercise medicine conference (UK) | Matched pre and post | 73 of 115 attendees, 16 universities | Seven author-developed Likert items | None | Matched data but non-validated items; authors note gains "may not directly reflect objective knowledge or competence" and small sample may inflate effects |
| Falola et al. 2025 [8], structured research training programme (11 African countries) | Pre and post | 223 enrolled, 152 completed both | **CRAI-12**, attitudes to research | None | Shows CRAI-12 works in medical students (alpha 0.97) but pre and post **could not be matched**, so analysed as independent samples |
| Bierer et al. 2015 [3], research-intensive medical school (US) | Matriculation vs graduation | 128 and 111 students | CRAI short form | Course length | CRAI distinguishes students with more research experience; single site |

Reviews that frame the field:

- **Lee et al. 2021** [9] (35 studies teaching research to medical students): 80% reported Kirkpatrick level 1 (reaction) outcomes, 17% level 2, and only one study level 4; most used internally designed questionnaires; studies often did not assess participants' prior research experience; "challenging to observe any enduring impact".
- **Mayne et al. 2024** [10] (27 studies of enablers and barriers): all cross-sectional; lack of time (20 studies), lack of training or information about opportunities (18) and access to mentors (11) were the commonest barriers; fewer than a quarter used valid measures; the authors note "the lack of well-controlled high-quality prospective studies".
- **Krungkraipetch et al. 2026** [11] (systematic review of research engagement in undergraduate medicine): heterogeneous outcomes and follow-up prevented synthesis; calls for equity-focused research on "whether research programs reduce or exacerbate disparities".
- **SMART, Parameswaran et al. 2023** [12] (1,772 students, 40 UK medical schools): 46.7% perceived barriers to research; the commonest was inadequate signposting (30.8%), then time (25.5%). Cross-sectional.

## 3.3 What this study adds

The study is built to answer two questions well:

1. **Gap 1: do changes after a student-led research conference show up in what students do?** It pairs a validated self-efficacy measure, measured before, immediately after and 8 weeks after, with six specific research actions, each asked about the 8 weeks before the conference and the 8 weeks after, in the same people. It is designed to keep follow-up (8 weeks rather than 6 months, inside the portal delegates already use, with consented reminders) so that the follow-up is analysable, which the closest previous study could not achieve.
2. **Gap 2: who changes most?** It prespecifies whether students with no research experience, or no mentor, change differently, reported as estimates with confidence intervals because the study is not powered to prove a difference.

The manuscript should frame INSPIRE as the setting and student-led research conferences as the intervention being studied.

# 4. Aims, questions and hypotheses

**Primary question.** Is attendance at a student-led research conference associated with a change in research self-efficacy from immediately before to immediately after?

- **H1 (primary):** mean CRAI-12 score is higher immediately after the conference than before.

**Key secondary questions** (tested in a fixed order, see 10.4):

- **H2 (Gap 1, behaviour):** the proportion of participants doing at least one of the six research actions is higher in the 8 weeks after the conference than in their own 8 weeks before it.
- **H3 (Gap 1, mechanism):** participants whose self-efficacy rose more, or who arranged to follow up with someone about research on the day, are more likely to report a research action at 8 weeks, adjusting for their research actions before the conference.

**Prespecified effect modification (Gap 2, estimation):** does the pre-to-post change differ between students with no previous research experience and those with some, and between students with and without a research mentor at baseline?

**Further secondary outcomes:** perceived access to research opportunities; interest in research as part of a future career; durability of changes at 8 weeks; current barriers; mentor status at 8 weeks.

**Exploratory:** programme activities, workshops and connections formed on the day; contact since the conference; free-text barriers.

# 5. Design

Prospective, multi-institutional, single-group observational cohort with three linked online questionnaires and a within-person comparison of two 8-week behaviour windows. Without a concurrent control group, the study can show changes **associated with** attendance, not changes **caused by** it, and every output must say so. Reporting will follow STROBE (cohort) [13] and CHERRIES (online surveys) [14].

| Questionnaire | Opens | Closes | Length |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1: Before | When the study is switched on | 09:00, 21 Nov 2026 (welcome session) | About 5 minutes (33 questions) |
| 2: After | 15:15, 21 Nov 2026 (before closing and prizes) | 23:59, 5 Dec 2026 | About 4 minutes (24 questions) |
| 3: Eight weeks on | 9 Jan 2027 (week 7) | 23:59, 30 Jan 2027 (week 10) | About 5 minutes (29 questions) |

Each questionnaire is kept to about five minutes (checked by an automated test), because length is one of the strongest predictors of attrition in online longitudinal surveys. Questions that did not serve a prespecified analysis were removed (see section 15, point 9). All windows are editable on the Research study page. People can join only while questionnaire 1 is open, so every participant has a baseline. Questionnaire 2 is opened in the closing session so delegates can answer before they leave; it should be announced from the stage with a QR code to My Hub.

# 6. Participants

**Eligible:** everyone with an approved or waitlisted delegate, poster presenter, oral presenter or oral waiting list account for INSPIRE 2026, aged 18 or over. Waitlisted applicants who do not attend are included: they answer questionnaire 2 ("Did you attend?" routes them past the conference-day questions) and form a small non-attending comparison group, reported descriptively.

**Not eligible:** organising committee members (including committee members who are also presenting), speakers and judges, who shape or deliver the intervention.

**The cohort (selected delegate list, 19 November 2026, aggregate only):** 232 people: 187 delegates, 36 poster presenters, 9 oral presenters. Universities: Lincoln 85 (37%), East Anglia 74 (32%), Nottingham 51 (22%), Leicester 11, Hull York 7, four others 1 each. Year of study: Year 1 57, Year 2 73, Year 3 34, Year 4 18, Year 5 19, foundation 4, intercalating 3, not recorded 24. More than half are in Years 1 and 2, which suits a study of students early in their research journey.

# 7. Recruitment, consent and withdrawal

- **Invitation:** the My Hub prompt and checklist item described at the top, plus an invitation email from the Research study page. No incentives.
- **Participant information and consent:** shown in full in My Hub (version-controlled, printable from the dashboard for the application). Five required statements (read the information, 18 or over, voluntary and no effect on their place, confidentiality, agree to take part) and three optional ones (email reminders; linking check-in on the day; depositing anonymised data). The consent version is stored with each decision.

- **Withdrawal:** at any time from the Research study page, keeping or deleting answers already given. Deletion is possible until the dataset is anonymised after questionnaire 3 closes.
- **Power relationships:** the organisers are students' peers. The data permission is off for every committee role and is given only to the named research team; the lead researcher named to participants should ideally be an academic outside the organising committee.

**Notes on the instruments.**

- **CRAI-12.** Chosen because it is validated, short, has been used with medical students [3, 8] and allows comparison with published work. Two of its six factors (funding; team planning) are less relevant to early-stage students and less likely to change after a one-day event, which could dilute the change. Prespecified sensitivity analysis: the 8-item score without the funding and planning factors. Report reliability (Cronbach's alpha and McDonald's omega) at each time point. **Before submission, confirm permission to use the CRAI-12** with the authors or through its listing in APA PsycTests, and cite it [15].
- **Perceived access scale.** New, so: run 5 to 8 cognitive interviews with students (ideally including Year 1 and widening participation students) before launch, report its internal consistency and a one-factor check, and describe it as developed for the study.
- **Not included, to keep each questionnaire to about five minutes:** a research literacy quiz, intention ratings, a single overall-confidence item and its retrospective "then-test", age group, previous conferences, a family link to medicine or research, number of students met, the most relevant part of the day, and whether the conference contributed to later actions. None was needed for H1 to H3 or the effect modification; most were exploratory or duplicated a retained measure, and the "did it contribute" item invites the attribution the design cannot support.

# 9. Sample size and power

The sample is fixed by the number of delegates, so this section estimates what the study can detect rather than how many to recruit.

**Response scenarios** (invited n = 232; post and 8-week rates are of those completing the baseline):

| Scenario | Baseline | After (paired) | 8 weeks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Optimistic (65% / 80% / 65%) | 151 | 121 | 98 |
| **Central (50% / 70% / 50%)** | **116** | **81** | **58** |
| Pessimistic, under 48 hours' notice (30% / 65% / 45%) | 70 | 45 | 31 |

The central scenario is set below Ali et al.'s 63% paired completion [7] because the baseline here needs action before the day, and well above Bligh et al.'s 11% 6-month retention [6] because the follow-up is at 8 weeks, inside My Hub, with reminders.

**Primary outcome (paired t-test, two-sided alpha 0.05, 80% power).** The smallest standardised paired change (d~z~) detectable:

| Paired n | 45 | 60 | 70 | 80 | 90 | 100 | 120 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Detectable d~z~ | 0.43 | 0.37 | 0.34 | 0.32 | 0.30 | 0.28 | 0.26 |

Required paired n for a given effect: d~z~ = 0.30 needs 90; 0.40 needs 52; 0.50 needs 34 (for 90% power: 119, 68 and 44). For a Wilcoxon signed-rank test, add about 15%. No published estimate of CRAI-12 change after a one-day event exists, so d~z~ = 0.3 (a small-to-moderate change) is taken as the smallest effect worth detecting. **The central scenario is adequately powered for the primary outcome; the pessimistic one is not.**

**Behaviour (H2, McNemar test).** Power depends on how many people change category. If 20% start an action they had not done before and 5% stop, about 85 pairs give 80% power; if 20% and 8%, about 150. With about 58 at 8 weeks, H2 is under-powered for modest changes and is reported mainly as a paired difference in proportions with its confidence interval.

**Effect modification (Gap 2).** Comparing change between two subgroups of 40 each, the detectable difference is about d = 0.63; with 30 and 50, 0.65. Real differences will be smaller, so subgroup results are reported as estimates with confidence intervals, not as hypothesis tests, and no subgroup conclusion will be drawn from a group of fewer than 20.

**How to describe the sample in the paper:** "a moderate-sized, multi-institutional cohort from a single conference". Not "large".

# 10. Statistical analysis plan

Register this plan (and the protocol) on the Open Science Framework before switching the study on, and certainly before any after-conference data are looked at. Analysis in R (lme4, lmerTest, mice, logistf).

## 10.1 Descriptive and response analyses

- A STROBE flow diagram: invited, decided, consented, each questionnaire completed, withdrawn.
- Baseline characteristics of participants, and of those completing each stage. Compare completers and non-completers on baseline scores and characteristics (attrition analysis).
- Compare participants with the invited population on university, year and role using aggregate counts from the delegate list (needs to be included in the ethics application).
- Reliability of each scale at each time point.

## 10.2 Primary analysis (H1)

Linear mixed model: CRAI-12 mean ~ time (before, after, 8 weeks) + random intercept for participant, using all available data (valid under missing at random). The primary estimate is the after-minus-before contrast with its 95% confidence interval. Effect sizes: d~z~ (mean change divided by the SD of change) and d~av~ (divided by the average of the before and after SDs), both reported.

Sensitivity analyses: (a) paired t-test and Wilcoxon signed-rank test on complete pairs; (b) multiple imputation (50 imputations) using baseline characteristics; (c) delta-adjusted pattern-mixture analysis, assuming non-responders changed less than responders, to find the "tipping point" at which the conclusion would change; (d) the 8-item CRAI without funding and planning items; (e) adding university as a fixed effect (too few universities for a random effect); (f) excluding waitlisted non-attenders.

## 10.3 Behaviour and mechanism (H2, H3)

- **H2:** proportion with at least one research action, 8 weeks before vs 8 weeks after, by McNemar's test, with the paired difference in proportions and its 95% confidence interval; number of actions (0 to 6) by Wilcoxon signed-rank. Sensitivity: excluding presenters (whose "before" window includes preparing their INSPIRE poster or talk), and excluding the two output items.
- **H3:** logistic regression (Firth's correction if events are few) of any action at 8 weeks on the change in CRAI-12 (before to after) and arranging to follow up on the day, adjusted for any action in the 8 weeks before and prior experience. To avoid over-fitting, no more than about one predictor per 10 events.

## 10.4 Multiplicity

H1 is tested at alpha 0.05. H2 and then H3 are tested in a fixed sequence, each only if the one before it is significant, which controls the family-wise error rate. Everything else is reported with 95% confidence intervals and labelled exploratory.

## 10.5 Effect modification (Gap 2)

Add time × no prior research experience, and separately time × no mentor at baseline, to the primary model; report each interaction and the change in each subgroup with 95% confidence intervals. Because subgroups defined by low baseline experience will start lower, regression to the mean will exaggerate their apparent gain; also report an ANCOVA of the after score adjusted for the before score.

## 10.6 Exploratory analyses

- **Workshops:** change in CRAI-12 by workshop attended (for example the critical-appraisal workshop "Don't Believe the Headline"). Workshop places followed ranking, so preference confounds this.
- **Programme parts and connections:** descriptive; associations with change are hypothesis-generating only.
- **Durability:** before vs 8-week contrast from the primary model.
- **Free text:** content analysis by two coders.
- **Non-attending waitlisted applicants:** descriptive only.

## 10.7 Data quality

Questionnaires cannot be submitted incomplete (except optional items), so item-level missing data are designed out; missing data are at questionnaire level. Completion time is exported; responses completed in under a third of the median time are flagged and checked in a sensitivity analysis.

# 11. Bias and limitations, and how the design limits them

| Threat | Effect | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| No control group | Changes may reflect time, term or other events | Within-person 8-week behaviour windows; co-intervention question; waitlisted non-attenders (descriptive); careful language ("associated with"). Strongest fix: repeat at further conferences. |
| Self-selection of delegates | Attenders are already interested in research | Describe the cohort fully; study change within people, not levels; report prior experience. Generalise to students who attend such conferences, not all students. |
| Self-selection of responders and attrition | Responders may change more | Attrition analysis; mixed models; multiple imputation; tipping-point analysis; reminders; short questionnaires. |
| Social desirability and demand | Inflated "after" scores | Separate from feedback; committee cannot see answers; neutral wording; "there are no right answers". |
| Response shift | People re-calibrate what "confident" means | Not measured (a then-test was dropped to keep questionnaires short) [16]; state as a limitation; the behavioural outcome is not affected. |
| Testing and question-behaviour effects | Answering about research actions can itself prompt action | Present as a limitation; it affects every participant equally; within-cohort comparisons. |
| Recall of the 8 weeks before | Under- or over-reporting | Specific, concrete actions; a defined window. |
| Season | The 8 weeks after include the Christmas break, which would reduce actions | Biases H2 towards no difference (conservative); state this. |
| Presenters | Their "before" window includes preparing for INSPIRE | Sensitivity analysis excluding presenters. |
| Single conference, three universities dominate | Limits generalisability | University adjustment; replicate at other conferences. |
| Self-reported behaviour | Not verified | Concrete yes/no actions; could add optional verification in a later round. |

# 12. Ethics and data governance

- **Approval:** from a university research ethics committee (normally the host university's). Participants come from several universities: check whether any require their own gatekeeper permission to survey their students.
- **Sponsor and lawful basis:** the sponsoring university's data protection officer should confirm the lawful basis (likely task in the public interest, with Article 9(2)(j) for any special category data) and complete or approve a data protection impact assessment. The participant information wording on this point must be checked with them.
- **How the website protects the data:**
  - answers are stored under a random study code (for example IN-7K3QX9); the link from code to account sits in a separate table used only to connect a person's questionnaires, send reminders and act on withdrawal;
  - nobody can read the research tables directly; everything goes through database functions that check permissions;
  - only holders of the new "Research study" permission (off for every role; given by name) can see counts or download data; the wider committee cannot;
  - exports contain study codes, never names, emails or account ids, and dates are converted to days from the conference; every export is written to the audit log;
  - after questionnaire 3 closes, the site owner deletes the code-to-account link permanently ("Anonymise the data"), after which the dataset is anonymous;
  - the study is off by default, and the database refuses to switch it on until an ethics committee, approval reference, approval date, data governance confirmation, lead researcher and valid windows are recorded.
- **Retention:** set by the sponsor (commonly 5 to 10 years for anonymised research data). The pseudonymised link is kept only until anonymisation.

# 15. Critical appraisal against the standard for a publishable original article

Reviewers in medical education commonly judge quantitative studies against domains like those of MERSQI [17]: design, sampling, type of data, validity of the instrument, analysis and outcome level.

| Domain | Where this study stands | What would strengthen it |
|---|---|---|
| Design | Single-group, three time points: better than one-group pre-post, below a controlled design | A comparison group (non-attending applicants in larger numbers, or a matched cohort from one medical school); replication at a second conference |
| Sampling | Multi-institutional, but one conference; response unknown until launch | Aim for at least 50% baseline response; report response at every stage |
| Data | Self-reported confidence and self-reported behaviour | Verify a subset of reported actions |
| Instrument validity | CRAI-12 validated; new access scale needs evidence | Cognitive interviews; report reliability and structure |
| Analysis | Prespecified, mixed models, sensitivity analyses | Preregister before launch |
| Outcome level | Reaches behaviour (Kirkpatrick level 3) | Longer follow-up in a later round |

**Weaknesses found during this review, and the changes made:**

1. *A long list of conference-specific outcomes would have read as an evaluation.* Narrowed to one primary outcome, a behavioural key secondary outcome and one prespecified effect modification, all framed around conferences in general.
2. *Self-efficacy alone would repeat what is known.* Added the 8-week behaviour windows (same person, before and after), which is the clearest gap.
3. *A 6-month follow-up would likely repeat Bligh et al.'s attrition.* Chose 8 weeks, in-portal, with consented reminders.
4. *Pre-test scores may be inflated before people understand the tasks.* A then-test was considered, then dropped for length; response shift is stated as a limitation.
5. *A literacy quiz would add an objective measure.* Dropped: exposure differed by workshop, re-testing confounds it, and it added two minutes for an exploratory outcome.
6. *Presenters bias the "before" behaviour window.* Added a sensitivity analysis.
7. *"Required" could invalidate consent.* Changed to "required to answer, free to decline".
8. *The sample could be oversold.* The protocol and dashboard state the power realistically.
9. *Long questionnaires lose participants, above all at follow-up.* Cut to about 5, 4 and 5 minutes (33, 24 and 29 questions) by removing every item that did not serve a prespecified analysis (listed in section 8). The primary outcome, behaviour, mechanism and effect-modifier questions are unchanged.
